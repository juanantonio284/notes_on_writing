# The Logistic Process of Writing a Book

[2nd draft: July 21, 2025]

## Introduction

This an exposition of the things that need to happen so that, quite pragmatically, the process of
writing a book does not become hell on the production team, and so that the final product is not
trash. 

This is a sort of map that

* marks the places where there should be communication between the parties (author, editor,
  copy-editor, typesetter, etc.), and 
* describes what the author should be doing at each stage.

It may sound conceited to talk about what the author should be doing. It's easy to imagine a
cartoonish evil editor working for corporate interests: censoring and placing unreasonable
deadlines on the pure-hearted artist writer. But I've learned that 'author' does **not**
mean "genius professional with a clear vision and clear voice"; it just means "the person doing
most of the writing". 

In real life: 

1. It's quite unlikely that you're working with a genius and, even if you are, it's quite unlikely
that this person is both a genius in his field and, also, a good communicator.

2. Having strong opinions, or having an "encyclopedic knowledge" on a subject, does not mean having
a clear vision. Almost every book starts with a strong opinion, but not every book is actually
completed—and of the books that are completed, how many are actually worth reading? It's a hard
lesson to learn but it is possible for someone with strong opinions, a good sales pitch, and ample
knowledge of a subject to be totally lost as to where a book should begin and where it should end.
<!-- all this does not translate to someone being able to sit down and put in the work to write properly. -->

3. Many writers do not have their own voice, they don't even have a plan of what they want to sound
like. They might be in thrall to the culture of their fields or schools, or to the ideas of
influential authors. (This is not a question of "originality", there's no need for a philosophical
discussion about where ideas come from or whether any idea can be truly original. This is about not
wasting time trying to fix a poorly written pastiche that says everything that's already been
said—and better—in an already published book.)

4. Many writers are not able to put in the work to actually finish a book—let alone a *good* book.
Even without bad intentions, they may just lack the motivation/energy/time to work on a book;
others might, for one reason or another, always want to research or polish "a little bit more" and
never actually publish. And this is without even taking into account that some people are just
selfish or corrupt and don't respect other's time and work. 
<!-- (If you are not in a formal setting, with money and contracts, be careful that writers don't think of you as their friend/companion.) -->
 
And so ... Since we can't always depend on authors being brilliant, professional, good at the craft
of writing, or having good intentions and respect for the people they work with, I present this
guide on the logistic process of writing a book. 

Is this guide perfect? No. Incomplete? Probably. But this is about survival: there is a point where
the writer is just not delivering to the point that you've sat in a chair, in front of a computer,
making corrections and having meetings for enough hours that health starts to suffer and medical
appointments need to be scheduled. Maybe one day you'll meet a brilliant artist—synthesizing ideas
from the aether and pouring them out in perfect prose, in a timely fashion—in which case this
guide—and most of the production team—will not be necessary. But, just in case, let's stick to a
plan.

## Process

### Author's own time 

Start whenever you want, take as long as you want, but this must be well developed before starting a
production process. (This is not trivial, it's not a checklist, it's not "how to write a book in 10
easy steps". This is *think about the work you should put in before you pretend to start writing a
book and involve other people*).

#### 1

* Gather knowledge

* Come up with the first idea (a spark, a seed)

* Do semi-specific research (go deeper into the idea and think about how/why it can turn into an
  article/paper/book)

* Write down a few sentences that explain where the idea comes from and where it's going in a
  theoretical/academic sense
    - By "write down", I mean write down (it shouldn't be in your head, you shouldn't expect to
      communicate it orally. Treat it like work: write it clearly, put a date on it, store it
      properly)
    - By "a few sentences" I mean, a few sentences (this is not an essay, it's not a treatise, it's
      not the first chapter of the book)

* Write down a few sentences that connect the idea to the world, in a practical sense: why do you
  want to write about this subject? What's the incentive for you? What's the benefit to others? Who
  will read it? How much work do you expect it will take? Have you done the research? Have you
  written about this before? How long do you expect each stage to take? How long do you expect the
  whole project to take?

#### 2: Hard Pre-Write

* Make a Table of Contents

* Have a clearly written, ranked, bibliography (latex/bibtex)

* Have a clear idea of what voice the book will be written in (will it sound like a textbook, a self
  help book, will you talk to the reader ...?)

### Editor Check 1

Discuss everything that the author's done in his/her own time
- See if you can expand the ToC a little bit (at the meeting), tell writer to go home and expand it
  anyway.
- Have a hard talk about whether the references/bibliography are suitable. This is the stage to talk
  about where the writer is getting his ideas from. 
- Talk about what voice the writer will write in.
