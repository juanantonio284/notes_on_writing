# The Logistic Process of Writing a Book

[3rd draft: February 18, 2026]

## Introduction

This an exposition of the things that need to happen so that, quite pragmatically, the process of
writing a book does not become hell on the production team, and so that the final product is not
trash. 

This is a sort of map to

* mark the places where there should be communication between the parties (author, editor,
  copy-editor, typesetter, etc.) and 
* describe what the author should be doing at each stage.

It may sound conceited to talk about what the author should be doing. It's easy to imagine a
cartoonish, evil, editor working for corporate interests—censoring and placing unreasonable
deadlines on the pure-hearted artist writer. But I've learned that 'author' does **not** mean 
"genius professional with a clear vision and clear voice"; it just means "the person doing most of 
the writing". 

In real life: 

1. It's quite unlikely that you're working with a genius and, even if you are, it's unlikely that
this person is both a genius in his field and, also, a good communicator.

2. Having strong opinions, or having an "encyclopedic knowledge" on a subject, does not mean having
a clear vision. Almost every book starts with a strong opinion, and many people with encyclopedic
knowledge want to write a book, but how many books are actually completed. And of the books that
are completed, how many are actually worth reading? It's a hard lesson to learn but it is possible
for someone with strong opinions, a good sales pitch, and ample knowledge of a subject to be
totally lost as to where a book should begin and where it should end.

3. Many writers do not have their own voice, they don't even have a plan of what they want to sound
like. They might be in thrall to the culture of their fields or schools, or to the ideas of
influential authors. (This is not a question of "originality", there's no need for a philosophical
discussion about where ideas come from or whether any idea can be truly original. This is, simply,
about not wasting time trying to fix a poorly written pastiche that says everything that's already
been said—and better—in an already published book.)

4. Many writers are not able to put in the work to actually finish a book—let alone a *good* book.
Even without bad intentions, an author may just lack the motivation/energy/time to work on a
book—that seems to be the rule rather than the exception. And this is without even taking into
account that some people are just selfish or corrupt and don't respect other's time and work. 
<!-- (If you are not in a formal setting, with money and contracts, be careful that writers don't think of you as their friend/companion.) -->
 
And so ... 

Since we can't always depend on authors being brilliant, professional, good at the craft of writing,
having motivation/energy/time, or respect for the people they work with, I present this guide on
the logistic process of writing a book. 

Is this guide perfect? No. Incomplete? Probably. But this is about survival: there is a point where
the writer is just not delivering to the point that you've sat in a chair, in front of a computer,
making corrections and having meetings for enough hours that health starts to suffer and medical
appointments need to be scheduled. (Work in tech for a couple years and tell me if that's an
exaggeration.) Maybe one day you'll meet a brilliant artist—synthesizing ideas from the aether and
pouring them out in perfect prose, in a timely fashion—in which case this guide—and most of the
production team—will not be necessary. But, just in case, let's stick to a plan.

## Process

### Author's own time 

Start whenever you want, take as long as you want, but this must be well developed before starting a
production process. (This is not trivial, it's not a checklist, it's not "how to write a book in 10
easy steps". This is *think about the work you should put in before you pretend to start writing a
book and involve other people*.)

#### 1

* Gather knowledge
* Come up with the first idea (a spark, a seed)
* Do semi-specific research (go deeper into the idea and think about how/why it can turn into an
  article/paper/book)
* Write down a few sentences that explain where the idea comes from and where it's going in a
  theoretical/academic sense
    - By "write down", I mean write down (it shouldn't be in your head, you shouldn't expect to
      communicate it orally. Treat it like work: write it clearly, put a date on it, store it
      properly)
    - By "a few sentences" I mean, a few sentences (this is not an essay, it's not a treatise, it's
      not the first chapter of the book)
* Write down a few sentences that connect the idea to the world, in a practical sense: why do you
  want to write about this subject? What's the incentive for you? What's the benefit to others? Who
  will read it? How much work do you expect it will take? Have you done the research? Have you
  written about this before? How long do you expect each stage to take? How long do you expect the
  whole project to take?

#### 2: Hard Pre-Write

These three simple things—that most authors "know" in their own mind—might take weeks to lay down on
paper and they are, hopefully, the result of years of work.

1. Have a clear idea of what voice the book will be written in (will it sound like a textbook, a self
  help book, will you talk to the reader ...?)

2. Have a clearly organized, and ranked, bibliography (at the very least made in markdown, if not
latex/bibtex). By the time someone's decided to write a book he/she should know what the
fundamental books and papers of the field are, which ones are more complete or more abridged, which
ones are more didactic, which ones are outdated, which ones hold strong, etc. 

3. Make a skeleton.

**Skeleton**

A skeleton is a sort of *Table of Contents* but going well beyond, down to sub-sub-sub-sections, and
more focused on clarifying what the content of each section is rather than giving a name to a
chapter. Think of it as a blueprint that clearly exhibits the ideas that will be treated and the
order in which the author wants to treat them.
  
For example, an author claims to want to write a book with "only two chapters: one on cats and one
on dogs". The table of contents might say "`Chapter 1: Cats` `Chapter 2: Dogs`". A skeleton, on the
other hand, would be like: 

```

Chapter 1: General information about cats
  Section: The complete history of cat domestication
  Section: Listing of cat breeds with pictures
  Section: A discussion of the most common cat breeds (comparisons between them, and breeding
  techniques)

Chapter 2: General information about dogs

```

In the example above, it becomes apparent that the "sections" are too expansive. They probably could
be books all by themselves so all sorts of decisions will need to be made. Should there be a
chapter, not a section, on how the cat was domesticated? Should it be conceived of as a "complete"
history? 

After a bit of psychology, you might realize that the author is actually passionate and
knowledgeable about cat breeding but thought, for whatever reason, that he should talk about all
the topics above when he really wanted to talk about "Classical Cat and Dog Breeding" [If there is
such a thing ... ] Does the author have clear whether he's writing a general interest book or a
very technical, specialized, book? 

Hopefully after many iterations of making a skeleton and discussing it, many questions will be
answered. In fact, it's not much of an exaggeration to say that writing the skeleton *is* the work.
Maybe an "architect's scale model" is a better analogy ...

### Editor Check 1

[Really, this might be check 1, 2, and 3]

Discuss everything that the author's done in his/her own time

1. Talk about what voice the writer will write in. 

2. Have a hard talk about whether the references are suitable and whether the bibliography is
organized enough.
  
  * This is the stage to talk about where the writer is getting his ideas from
  * The bibliography should be clear to everyone (not in the author's head, and not in his personal
    notebook). The bibliography is what the writing/editing team will use as reference throughout
    the whole process—months or years—of writing the book. You do not want to get to a late stage,
    and have alarms going off: "Wait, are we using definition A from book X, or definition B from
    book Y? Oh, you put them together and made your own. That's nice. When exactly did that
    happen?"

3. See if you can expand on the skeleton and understand/clarify every header; regardless of how good
it is, it's almost certain the writer should go home and re-check it.

  * Every header in the skeleton should leave clear what the idea in the section/chapter is and
    should serve to make a section/chapter title
