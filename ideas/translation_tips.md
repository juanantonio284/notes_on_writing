# Tips I Would Give My Younger Self For Doing Translation Work

## Social/Professional

1. It's not "a translation", it's work. *You* have to treat it as a job (have clear rules and enough
payment) because no-one else will. People think it's just about switching words from one language
to another and don't see much difference between doing what you do and copying/pasting in an online
translator.

    * Ask *why* they feel they need a translator versus translating it themselves using an online
      translator, or asking friend who "speaks English". This should help them verbalize what they
      expect from you and realize what the job might entail

1. Tell the author that he'll likely have to do extensive corrections before a translation can be
done.
    * give a quick estimate of the time and work it will take to make those corrections
    * if the author thinks his work is a fully finished masterpiece, that he's a master in his own
      language, and that he "only" needs a translation to English do not be surprised (this is
      probably what's going to happen). Do not work with people like this. Flip burgers or bag
      groceries—it pays better and allows for more intellectual growth. Boy does the saying "not
      all money is good money" apply here ...

1. If the author has a good attitude and actually wants to have a quality product, offer to help
with those corrections as *separate work for separate payment* before a translation is started.
(A full copy-editing process is probably needed)

## Work Specific

1. Have the author decide on a style and correct his writing before having others work on the copy 
    * I've found that people mix writing styles (formal, colloquial, academic) all in the same
      document. Not sure if this is my bad luck, a localized cultural problem, or something that
      writers of every culture do ...

1. Have the author standardize terminology (capitalization, italics, conjunctions and prepositions)
    * For example, pick one: "casa de cartas", "casa de naipe", "casa de naipes"

1. Determine which terms have a deeper meaning and allocate time for a separate process where:
    * terms are properly standardized (capitalization, italics)
    * terms are translated in a way that makes sense (author's input is heavily required)
    * e.g. If the author says "... estado Mexicano", does he mean a state/province of Mexico, the
      Federal Government, or the supervising agent of social order?
      - No, you shouldn't simply try to infer this from the context and "keep it moving". When the
        topics are complex and/or the authors are "intellectuals"[^note], it's likely that terms
        are overused or defined loosely and their meaning changed throughout as it suits the
        author's agenda

1. Re-write for sense and basic grammar. Correct:
    * grammar,
    * style problems like run-on lists, headers, and poor use of punctuation,
    * explanations within sentences (the "sentence" winds up being the length of a page), 
    * explanations that don't properly close (the writer provided something like an explanation or a
      reference to another author) but has not actually made a complete argument,
    * etc.
    
1. After you can understand what the document says in one language (you know, because the author has
actually done his job) you begin translating to another language.

[^note]: Yes, I mean 'intellectuals' in the most sarcastic way possible.
