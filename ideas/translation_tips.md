# Tips I Would Give My Younger Self For Doing Translation Work

## Social/Professional

1. It's not a gig, it's a full-time job. *You* have to treat it as a job because no-one else will,
so have clear rules and enough pay to make it worth the time and effort. 

    * People think it's just about switching words from one language to another and don't see much
      difference between doing what you do and copying/pasting in an online translator

    * Ask *why* they feel they need a translator versus translating it themselves using an online
      translator. This should help them verbalize what they expect from you and realize what the
      job might entail. (Beware they think of you as a friend who "speaks English and can translate
      easily".)

1. Tell the author that he'll likely have to do extensive corrections before a translation can be
done.

    * A full copy-editing process is usually needed unless the work has already been professionally
      published—and even then, it might still be needed ...
      
    * If the author thinks his work is a fully finished masterpiece, that he's a master in his own
      language, and that he "only" needs a translation to English do not be surprised—this is
      common. Do not work with people like this. Flip burgers or bag groceries—it pays better and
      allows for more intellectual growth. (Boy, does the saying "not all money is good money"
      apply here ... )

1. Read the text and give the author an estimate of the time and work it will take to make
corrections. Everyone should be clear on when a translation can actually start.

    * If the author has a good attitude and actually wants to have a quality product, and you want
      to help with those corrections, offer to help as *separate work for separate payment, with
      separate deadlines and expectations*


## Work Specific

1. Have the author decide on a style and correct his writing before having others work on the copy 
    
    * People often mix writing styles (formal, colloquial, academic) all in the same document. Not
      sure if this is my bad luck, a localized problem, or something that writers of every culture
      do ...

1. Have the author standardize terminology (capitalization, italics, conjunctions, and prepositions)
    
    * For example, pick one: "casa de cartas", "casa de naipe", "casa de naipes"

1. Determine which terms have a deeper meaning and allocate time for a separate process where:
    
    * terms are standardized (capitalization, italics), and 
    * terms are translated in a way that makes sense (The author's input is heavily required here)
        - For example: if the author says "... estado Mexicano", does he mean a state/province of
          Mexico, the Federal Government, or the supervising agent of social order? Was he careful
          about the use of capitals in the word 'estado'? (Probably not)
            - You shouldn't always try to infer the meaning from the context and "keep it moving".
              When the topics are complex and/or the authors are "intellectuals"[^note], it's
              likely that terms are overused or defined loosely and their meaning changed
              throughout as it suits the author's agenda

1. Re-write for sense and basic grammar. Correct:
    * grammar
    * style problems like run-on lists, headers, and poor use of punctuation
    * explanations within sentences (the author inserts all sorts of qualifiers and caveats in an
      idea and the "sentence" winds up being longer than a paragraph), 
    * explanations that don't properly close (the writer provided something like an explanation or a
      reference to another author) but has not actually made a complete argument,
    * other things ...
    
1. After you can understand what the document says in one language (i.e. after the writer has
actually done his job) you can begin translating to another language.

[^note]: Yes, I mean 'intellectuals' in the most sarcastic way possible.
