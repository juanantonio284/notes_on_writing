# The Logistic Process of Writing a Book

[1st draft: July 20, 2025]

## Introduction

This an exposition of the things that need to happen so that, quite pragmatically, the process of writing a book does not become hell on the production team, and so that the final product is not trash. 

This is a sort of map that

* marks the places where there should be communication between the parties (author, editor,
  copy-editor, typesetter, etc.), and 
* describes what the author should be doing at each stage.

It may sound conceited to speak of what the author should do. It's easy to imagine a cartoonish evil editor working for corporate interests: censoring and placing unreasonable deadlines on the pure-hearted artist writer. But I've learned that in real life, 'author' does **not** mean "genius professional with a clear vision and clear voice"; it just means "the person doing most of the writing". 

1. It's quite unlikely that you're working with a genius and, even if you are, it's quite unlikely that this person is both genius in his field and, also, a good communicator.

2. Having strong opinions does not mean having a clear vision. Almost everyone that's written a book has a strong opinion on something (that's why they take the time and energy to write a book in the first place); but there's plenty of books that go nowhere. Strong opinions, good sales pitches, and ample knowledge of a subject do not translate to good writing. 

3. Many authors do not have their own voice, they don't even have a plan of what they want to sound like. They might be in thrall to the culture of their fields or schools, or to the ideas of influential authors. 

4. Some authors are not willing to put in the work to actually finish a book (let alone a *good* book). If you are not in a formal setting, with money and contracts, be careful that writers don't think of you as their friend/companion. Even with no bad intentions, some writers constantly lack confidence and just want to research or polish "a little bit more", other writers just don't have the motivation/energy/schedule to work on the book. Then there's people that are just selfish or corrupt and don't mind wasting everyone's time ...
 
And so ... Since we can't always depend on authors being brilliant, professional, good at the craft of writing, or having good intentions and respect for the people they work with, I present this guide on the logistic process of writing a book. 

Is this guide perfect? No. Incomplete? Probably. But this is about survival: there is a point where the writer is just not delivering to the point that you've sat in a chair, in front of a computer, making corrections and having meetings for enough hours that health starts to suffer and medical appointments need to be scheduled. Maybe you'll meet a brilliant artist, synthesizing ideas from the aether and pouring them out in perfect prose, in which case this guide will not be necessary. But, just in case, let's stick to a plan.

## Process

### Life

gathering knowledge
seed of an idea
semi-specific research
idea a little further developed and connected to the real world (schedule, logistics, incentive)

### Hard Pre-write

Table of Contents
Bibliography
Strong determination of what voice the book will be written in (textbook, direct to reader, etc?)

### Editor Check 1

Discuss the things in the hard Pre-write
- See if you can expand the ToC a little bit (at the meeting), tell writer to go expand anyway.
- Have a hard talk about whether the references/bibliography are suitable. This is the stage to talk
  about where the writer is getting his ideas from. 
- Talk about what voice the writer will write in.
